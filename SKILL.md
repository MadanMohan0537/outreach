# Startup founder cold outreach

Goal: land conversations with startup founders directly, skipping job boards. At startups the founder usually does the hiring and many roles never get posted. Distilled from [@textbackdoor](https://x.com/textbackdoor) (Backdoor) posts and their blog post [The 15-minute cold email](https://backdoorai.app/blog/the-15-minute-cold-email). Last refreshed: 2026-11-17.

Also drawn from [cold email templates](https://backdoorai.app/cold-email-templates), [how to cold email a founder](https://backdoorai.app/blog/how-to-cold-email-a-startup-founder), [how to follow up](https://backdoorai.app/blog/follow-up-email-founder-no-reply), [interview guide](https://backdoorai.app/blog/how-to-land-interviews-at-early-stage-startups), [most startup jobs are never posted](https://backdoorai.app/blog/most-startup-jobs-are-never-posted), [OPT guide](https://backdoorai.app/guides/cold-email-founder-opt), [founder reply rates](https://backdoorai.app/research/founder-reply-rates), and the [Nov 17, 2026 playbook post](https://x.com/textbackdoor/status/2122521297192411398). Dated source quotes live in `notes.md`. This file is the current method.

## Ground rules for the assistant

- Prepare drafts for the person to review and send. Never send on the person's behalf without their explicit go-ahead.
- Never invent proof, metrics, or a personal connection. Every claim in a draft comes from the person's real work or from a public fact about the startup, with its source link.
- Never guess or infer a private email address. Use only an address that was published, and record where it was found.

## 1. Pick startups (quality over volume)

- 10 relevant startups beat 500 generic ones. Start with startups in the same domain as work the person has already done.
- Favor a fresh signal: raised money or grew this year, a recent hiring post, or a founder post about a problem the person has solved before.
- Niche roles where the person clearly fits get the best reply rates; a basic profile is fine if the fit is specific.
- If the person needs visa sponsorship, prioritize startups that have publicly said they sponsor (H-1B filings, job posts, founder statements), and record the source.
- November is a strong month: bonus season churn leaves openings.
- Skip any founder you can't write a real, specific reaction to.

Small startups are the usual fit, because the founder still reads their own inbox and makes the hiring call. The account describes that range as pre-seed through Series C, often under 30 to 50 people. Useful places a fresh signal shows up:

- Funding news, including a seed or Series A in the last 90 days.
- A recent YC batch marked as hiring (`ycombinator.com/companies`).
- A launch on Product Hunt, Hacker News, LinkedIn, or X.
- A founder post that says "we're hiring", "stretched thin", or "know anyone who...".
- A careers page that just appeared. Use it only as a signal. The email still goes to the founder.

For each company, record the signal and its source, the founder's name, what they shipped recently, why it fits, and one thing the person could take off the founder's plate.

## 2. Find the founder's email

- Email the founder, not HR or a careers inbox. Skip application forms.
- Use only published addresses: the founder's site, X or LinkedIn bio, company team or contact page, talks, GitHub, or public founder lists shared on Reddit or X. Launch posts, "Show HN" notes, and podcast show notes count when the address is printed there. Record where it was found. Never guess or infer a private address.
- A warm intro is worth using when one exists. Check who the person already knows at the company, ask "do you know anyone at [company]?", and give that person two lines they can forward. Cold email is the path when there is no intro.
- If no published founder address turns up, skip that founder. Do not fall back to `careers@`, a hiring inbox, or a patterned guess such as `firstname@company.com`.

## 3. Write the first email (about 15 minutes, no endless polishing)

- Open with them: the first line references their recent post, launch, or product, sent the same day or within a day or two while it's fresh. If they haven't posted, use a concrete thing you noticed using their product (a bug, friction point) with a link to evidence.
- One real reaction ("I hit this exact thing last month"), never "love what you're building".
- Under 100 words. Phone-readable with no scrolling.
- One thing the person made, the closest to the founder's problem, with a link. Not the whole CV.
- One number ("6s to 1.8s", "lifted signups 22%") instead of adjectives.
- Sound like a text to someone you respect, not a cover letter. No "I am writing to express my interest".
- The preview line is the pitch: the first words should show relevance, not "hey, how's it going".
- A link, not a PDF attachment. If they want the resume they'll ask.
- A small proof-of-work idea (a mockup, a fix, a quick analysis) strengthens it a lot. It stays small enough that the email still goes out in about 15 minutes. Host it at a link. Examples:
  - **Engineer:** a failing test plus a fix for one reproduced issue, or a small feature prototype.
  - **Designer:** the signup flow with a few places people get stuck.
  - **Growth or marketing:** a short run of posts, or a list of accounts that look like their best customers.
  - **Sales or ops:** a short list of companies that should buy, with the reason for each.
  - **Product or project manager:** a one-page teardown covering the problem, what to change, and how to measure it.
- Ask exactly one yes/no question ("Would you be open to 10 minutes this week?" / "open to me working on this?"). Never stack asks.
- Subject: specific and about them, e.g. "Your post on signup drop-off" or "Coupon bug in [product] checkout".
- Send from the person's own email address.
- If status needs to be mentioned (OPT, F-1, H-1B), add one factual line after the proof. Keep it out of the subject, and don't apologize for it. Prefer startups already recorded as publicly sponsoring.

Shape: Hi [name], [their post/product + real reaction]. [one specific observation or idea]. At [company], I [one thing built] and [one number]. [one yes/no ask]. [sender]

## 4. Follow up once

- Day 4, same thread, add something new (another finding, a mockup, a data point). Never "just checking in". One follow-up only.
- Stop when they reply. After a no, send the thank-you and intro ask in section 5, then stop.
- Keep the follow-up in the same thread. Leave out a next-day nudge, a new subject line, a guilt-trip, and a re-attached resume. One LinkedIn connection request with a short note is fine; messaging every channel at once is not.

## 5. Handle replies

- Yes: accept, say what you'd tackle first, ask start date, compensation, and who you'd work with. Reply quickly; founders move fast and forget faster. A rough sketch of the first month is a good way to show what you'd tackle first. Settle pay, hours, deliverables, and the decision date before any trial work.
- No: thank them and ask for one intro to another founder working on the same problem, with a two-line forwardable blurb.
- Rude reply: close it and move to the next one.

## 6. Cadence and tracking

- Send a few every day, consistently, rather than big batches. Write while the signal is fresh (the same day or within a day or two). In Backdoor's data, the first person to email a founder gets about 3 times the replies of the fifth (5.8% against 2.0%).
- Track each startup: signal and source, founder, email and source, proof-work idea, sent date, follow-up due (day 4), status (drafted, sent, followed up, replied yes/no, closed).
- Never send on the person's behalf without their explicit go-ahead; prepare drafts for them to review and send.

For each startup, the draft packet is: the company and the hiring signal with its link, the founder's name and the published email with where it was found, the proof-work idea, the first-email draft, and the day-4 follow-up draft.

## Avoid

- "I hope this email finds you well", "I am writing to express my interest", and "how's it going?"
- "Love what you're building" with no specific reaction.
- "Passionate, quick learner, team player" with no proof.
- A life story, or a list of every project.
- A resume PDF as the whole email.
- Several asks in one email.
- A template with only the name swapped.
- The same email sent in a batch, and auto-apply or spray-and-pray applications.
- "Respected Sir/Ma'am". Use the founder's first name.

## What to expect

Backdoor's Nov 17, 2026 snapshot: about 1 in 13 founders reply (971 replies from 13,016 first emails), and about 1 in 3 of those ask for a call. In that note, 50 emails lined up with about 4 replies and 1 or 2 calls, and about 4 weeks of sending lined up with about 15 replies and 5 calls. One yes is the point. In the same data, 2 in 3 founder replies came after a follow-up (619 of 971), which is why the day-4 note stays in the plan. An earlier research page said 9 in 10 first replies come within two weeks and about 3 in 10 come after a follow-up; the newer 2-in-3 figure comes from a larger set, so prefer it. One published run went from 76 emails to 14 replies to 3 calls to 1 offer. In the account's own example, a 186-word email got told off and a 69-word one got an offer. Silence on most emails is normal.
